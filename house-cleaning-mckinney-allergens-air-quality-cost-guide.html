Why Indoor Air Matters for House Cleaning in McKinney

Think of a closed lunchbox. Whatever goes in stays in until someone opens it and cleans it out. A home works the same way, especially a tightly built one.

The EPA says Americans spend about 90 percent of their time indoors, where some pollutants run 2 to 5 times higher than typical outdoor levels. EPA Region 1 adds that indoor levels can occasionally be more than 100 times higher, and that buildings designed to limit outdoor air leakage can hold high pollutant levels unless they have special mechanical ventilation.

Dust, pollen, pet dander, and cooking residue have nowhere to go. They settle on floors, fabrics, and vents until someone removes them. Cleaning is the main way those particles leave your home.

McKinney’s Year-Round Allergy Calendar

North Texas has no real off-season. Local physicians describe cedar pollen in winter, tree pollen in spring, grass pollen in summer, and ragweed in fall. A McKinney pollen forecast shows the same pattern: cedar and elm from January, oak through March and April, grass peaking in May and June, and ragweed into October.

Early November is an overlap. Ragweed lasts until the first freeze, which comes on average around November 22, and mountain cedar follows close behind.

Indoor allergens never take a season off. A Dallas allergy guide notes that dust mites like warm, humid places such as bedding, upholstery, and carpets.

The Cleaning Products Question: What Research Says

Some research suggests that the products used to clean a home can affect breathing.

  • A study of 3,503 asthma-free adults in 10 European countries found that aerosol cleaning sprays were linked to higher risk of new asthma symptoms (relative risk 1.49). Using four or more sprays a week raised the risk of diagnosed asthma (RR 2.11). No risk was found for products that were not sprayed.
  • In the Canadian CHILD cohort, children in homes with the most frequent cleaning product use had 37% higher odds of an asthma diagnosis, with scented, spray-on products showing the strongest link. The researchers note this shows an association, not proof of cause.
  • By age three, 7.9 percent of children in high-use homes had asthma, compared with nearly five percent in low-use homes.

This does not mean you should stop cleaning. It means you should ask which products are used. Fine-mist sprays and heavy fragrance are the recurring concern, so ask for fragrance-free or non-aerosol options if you have infants, asthma, or allergies. The same report notes that some “green” products can also contain harmful substances, so the label alone is not proof of safety.

One more caution. An older medical-news review reported that the only controlled study on steam-cleaning carpets found no allergen benefit. That evidence is dated, but it is a good reason to ask for proof before paying for extras.

How Often Should You Clean?

  • Weekly: Best for pets, young children, or allergy sufferers. Shorter visits keep dust and dander from building up.
  • Biweekly: The most common choice and a good middle ground.
  • Monthly: Fine for low-traffic homes, though visits take longer.
  • Seasonal deep cleans: One before cedar season (late fall) and one after peak tree and grass pollen (early summer).
  • New builds and moves: Many McKinney homes are recent builds, so a post-construction or move-in clean removes fine drywall dust and builder debris before you start a regular schedule.

Choosing a Cleaning Provider: What to Check

The McKinney market is crowded. One directory alone lists 59 local house cleaners. A few checks help you sort them:

  1. Insurance. One pricing guide notes that Texas does not require state licensing for house cleaners, so insurance is your main protection. Ask for proof.
  2. Vetting and consistency. Ask about background checks and whether you can request the same cleaner each time.
  3. Products. Ask what is used, and whether scented or aerosol products can be skipped.
  4. Scope in writing. Get the checklist for standard versus deep cleaning.
  5. Clear pricing. Choose providers that show the full price before you book.

A Simple Allergy-Smart Cleaning Plan

  • Vacuum with a sealed-system HEPA vacuum, focusing on carpets and rugs.
  • Wet-dust instead of dry-dusting so particles are captured, not spread around.
  • Wash bedding in hot water weekly during peak seasons.
  • Wipe window sills, blinds, and entry mats, where pollen collects first.
  • Keep windows shut on high-pollen days and run the A/C, as local allergy guidance recommends.
  • Use a HEPA air purifier in bedrooms and change HVAC filters on schedule.
  • Wipe pets after outdoor time so pollen and dander do not travel through the house.
